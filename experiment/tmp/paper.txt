Speciﬁcation-Driven Harmonic Navigation:
Risk- and Time-Certiﬁed Potential Field Synthesis
Doyoung Heo
Abstract
We study the inverse design of harmonic navigation ﬁelds from user-speciﬁed collision-risk and
arrival-deadline requirements. An occupancy-grid maze is converted into a family of harmonic
functions by solving Laplace boundary-value problems; their linear combination deﬁnes an ofﬂine-
synthesized gradient controller. For a ﬁrst-order robot subject to isotropic Brownian disturbances,
collision and ﬁnite-horizon safe-arrival probabilities satisfy linear backward equations for any ﬁxed
controller. We develop (i) a conservative collision-risk condition afﬁne in the harmonic coefﬁcients,
(ii) an automatically computable exponential exit-time certiﬁcate obtained from an eigenvalue prob-
lem and a sparse linear solve, and (iii) feasibility screens based on Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equa-
tions and parameter-space bounds. We distinguish exact probability evaluation, conservative cer-
tiﬁcates, and veriﬁed impossibility claims. The results establish a rigorous problem formulation
and a concrete algorithmic blueprint; efﬁciency improvements and tightness comparisons remain
research objectives rather than established empirical ﬁndings.
Keywords: harmonic navigation, stochastic reach–avoid, ﬁrst-passage time, boundary control,
PDE-constrained synthesis, feasibility certiﬁcation.
1. Introduction
Motion planning is a central problem in robotics: ﬁnding a path that allows a robot to move from
an initial position to a goal without colliding with obstacles. A broad range of methods has been
developed to optimize path length and motion efﬁciency. In real environments, however, external
disturbances and motion uncertainty can cause a robot to deviate from its planned path, affecting
both collision outcomes and arrival times. Geometric path safety must therefore be complemented
by an assessment of the probability of reaching the goal safely within a prescribed time .
A variety of approaches has been studied for planning and controlling robot motion. Potential-
ﬁeld navigation determines motion directions from the gradient of a scalar function encoding the
goal and obstacles ( Khatib, 1986). Control barrier functions (CBFs) impose safety constraints on
control inputs to prevent entry into unsafe regions ( Ames et al. , 2017). Learning-based approaches
such as Diffusion Policy generate action sequences conditioned on observations through a diffusion
model (Chi et al., 2023). These approaches offer different strengths in generating motion directions,
enforcing safety, and learning complex behaviors. Nevertheless, constructing and verifying a con-
troller that simultaneously satisﬁes prescribed collision-probability and arrival-time requirements
remains a distinct design problem.
This motivates the following question: given an environment and a model of the robot’s motion
uncertainty, can we directly design a controller that satisﬁes speciﬁed collision-probability and
arrival-time requirements? We treat these requirements as inputs to controller design. Rather than
ﬁrst constructing a controller and then evaluating its performance, we seek a controller from user-
speciﬁed safety and deadline requirements. We also aim to identify cases in which the requirements
cannot be met because of the robot’s speed limit or the structure of the environment.

To this end, we consider controller design based on harmonic potentials. A nonconstant
harmonic potential has no interior local minima in a connected maze domain, eliminating local-
minimum traps away from the goal ( Connolly et al. , 1990; Connolly, 1993). Saddle points may
remain, so this property alone does not guarantee goal convergence. Moreover, linear combinations
of harmonic potentials remain harmonic. We can therefore construct a family of harmonic potentials
from the maze geometry and vary the induced motion directions by adjusting their combination co-
efﬁcients. The mathematical structure of harmonic potentials also allows probabilistic collision-risk
certiﬁcates to be derived under the stochastic disturbance model considered here. We exploit these
properties to search for a potential ﬁeld satisfying safety and deadline requirements by adjusting a
small set of coefﬁcients , rather than designing a general spatial control function directly.
Speciﬁcally, we ﬁrst construct a parameterized family of harmonic potentials from the maze
geometry. For each candidate ﬁeld, we analyze the robot’s stochastic motion to compute the prob-
ability of wall collision and the probability of safely reaching the goal within the deadline. These
evaluations guide optimization of the ﬁeld coefﬁcients to satisfy both requirements, followed by
veriﬁcation of the resulting controller. We further analyze performance limits over the class of
speed-limited controllers to obtain criteria for certifying when the prescribed requirements are im-
possible to achieve.
The main contributions of this work are as follows:
1. Speciﬁcation-driven potential-ﬁeld synthesis. We present a method for automatically con-
structing harmonic potentials from a given maze geometry and representing a family of con-
trollers through a small set of design variables.
2. Controller design with probabilistic safety and deadline requirements. We formulate
potential-ﬁeld synthesis using collision probabilities and ﬁnite-horizon safe-arrival probabili-
ties to address both requirements jointly. We also derive conditions for certifying probabilistic
performance from the mathematical properties of harmonic potentials.
3. Feasibility assessment of prescribed requirements. We provide conditions for verifying
that a candidate controller satisﬁes the requirements and criteria for mathematically certifying
that they cannot be achieved under the given environment and speed limit.
2. Related Work
2.1. Artiﬁcial potential ﬁelds and harmonic navigation
Khatib’s artiﬁcial potential ﬁelds introduced an inﬂuential reactive navigation mechanism: attraction
to the goal and repulsion from obstacles are encoded in a spatial potential, whose gradient generates
the instantaneous command ( Khatib, 1986). The approach is simple to execute but can introduce
unintended local minima. Rimon and Koditschek developed navigation functions with stronger
geometric and convergence properties, notably almost-global convergence under their stated con-
ditions (Rimon and Koditschek , 1992). Such results concern a carefully constructed deterministic
navigation structure and do not directly provide stochastic deadline probabilities.
Connolly, Burns, and Weiss proposed solving Laplace’s equation for path planning ( Connolly
et al. , 1990). Connolly subsequently detailed harmonic functions for robot navigation, including
smooth paths and reactive control (Connolly, 1993). The maximum principle excludes interior local
extrema for a nonconstant harmonic potential, but critical saddle points may remain; the property

2.2. Parameterized and customizable harmonic ﬁelds
Optimizing harmonic ﬁelds is not new. Wang, Guo, and Guo investigated customization over ho-
motopy classes by combining discrete geometric choices and continuous ﬁeld weights ( Wang et al.,
2025). Nicu, Stoican, Ioan, and Prodan proposed harmonic surfaces with B-spline Dirichlet data on
a polyhedral cell decomposition, creating safe transitions through a prescribed cell sequence ( Nicu
et al., 2026). These methods motivate our use of a compact boundary basis and show that tunable
boundary conditions should not be claimed as a standalone novelty. The primary difference is the
speciﬁcation: rather than requesting a homotopy class or a cell sequence, we prescribe collision
and deadline probabilities. The optimization variables represent boundary potentials, whereas the
objectives and constraints arise from the stochastic dynamics.
2.3. Collision probabilities and stochastic certiﬁcates
Connolly’s analysis relates harmonic boundary problems to random-walk hitting probabilities ( Con-
nolly, 1997). For a diffusion with an additional drift induced by a potential, hitting probabilities
generally satisfy a drifted backward equation, not the same Laplace equation as the generating
potential. Our treatment keeps this distinction explicit and uses harmonicity to construct an expo-
nential martingale for a conservative risk bound.
Stochastic barrier certiﬁcates furnish probability bounds for unsafe-state entry, often without
enumerating trajectories; Prajna, Jadbabaie, and Pappas used supermartingale-type conditions and
convex methods for appropriate system classes ( Prajna et al., 2004). Reach–avoid supermartingales
have also been used to learn and certify policies for stochastic systems ( Žikeli´c et al. , 2023). Thus,
joint stochastic reachability and safety certiﬁcation are established ideas. Our proposed distinction
is a low-dimensional harmonic-boundary parameterization that makes some sufﬁcient conditions
afﬁne in the coefﬁcients and produces an ofﬂine gradient controller without an online safety-ﬁltering
quadratic program. This does not imply an absence of barrier-like certiﬁcates in the analysis.
2.4. Arrival deadlines and feasibility
Masoud developed space–time harmonic navigation with obstacle avoidance, speed limits, and
prescribed arrival times, including feasibility-dependent motion ( Masoud, 2023). Related proba-
bilistic temporal reach–avoid–stay frameworks use spatiotemporal tubes and formal chance con-
straints ( Upadhyay et al. , 2026). Hence, neither explicit time requirements nor feasibility-aware
navigation is new by itself. Our focus is speciﬁcally a static harmonic ﬁeld that must meet a joint
stochastic collision/deadline speciﬁcation.
The necessary feasibility screens below are instances of stochastic optimal-control dynamic
programming, for which HJB and viscosity solution theory are standard ( Fleming and Soner, 2006).
The sparse discretization follows continuous-time Markov-chain approximation principles (Kushner
and Dupuis, 2001). A central unresolved question is whether this structured controller class enables
materially faster or less conservative certiﬁed synthesis than general stochastic approaches.

3. Problem Setup
3.1. Maze geometry and absorbing boundaries
Consider a binary occupancy grid O ∈ { 0, 1}nx×ny , with nx,n y ∈ N grid dimensions and grid
spacingh> 0 meters. V alue1 denotes an occupied cell; value 0 denotes free space. After inﬂating
occupied cells by the robot footprint, let F ⊂ R2 denote the continuous free space. Let G ⊂ F be
a target region with positive area, and let D ⊂ R2 be the bounded connected component of F \ G
that contains the initial position.
The boundary of D is partitioned into a wall/collision boundary ΓW and a goal boundary ΓG.
For the analysis we assume they are disjoint, sufﬁciently smooth boundary components and ∂D =
ΓW ∪ ΓG. An actual pixel maze must be smoothed or treated as a numerical approximation; its
corners do not automatically satisfy all smooth-boundary assumptions. Let x0 ∈ D be the initial
robot position. Both boundary components are absorbing: the trial ends at the ﬁrst contact with
either one.

3.5. Regularity assumptions and the meaning of stability
Assumption 2 (Well-posed stopped diffusion) The bounded connected domainD has sufﬁciently
smooth boundary; the target and wall boundary components are disjoint. Each basis potential is
C2(D) with a bounded, sufﬁciently regular gradient up to the boundary, and σ > 0 is constant.
The resulting stopped SDE is well posed, and the exit time is almost surely ﬁnite. Classical PDE
statements may be understood in the corresponding weak/viscosity sense if the required regularity
is relaxed.
Here safety means a bounded wall-hitting probability, and timely reachability means a lower bound
on deadline success. Harmonicity does not imply Lyapunov stability of the stochastic dynamics
around a goal point. In fact, the nondegenerate Brownian disturbance prevents hard, probability-
one obstacle avoidance in general. The absence of spurious interior extrema also does not exclude
saddle points of a deterministic gradient ﬂow.
4. Method: Feasibility-Aware Inverse Harmonic Synthesis
We separate ﬁeld construction, probability evaluation, certiﬁcate construction, and feasibility anal-
ysis. This separation is essential: a computable upper bound is a sufﬁcient certiﬁcate, whereas the
exact backward equations evaluate the chosen stochastic model and need not use that bound.
4.1. From a maze to a harmonic basis
SelectM sufﬁciently smooth boundary basis functions ψi : ∂D → R,i = 1,...,M . We choose
ψi = 0 on ΓG and allow different values over wall segments. Each basis potential is the harmonic
extension of its boundary data:
∆Vi(x) = 0 ( x ∈D), V i(w) = ψi(w) ( w ∈∂D). (4.1)
The value 0 on the goal is merely a gauge convention: adding a global constant to a potential does
not change its gradient. Wall boundary values are free coefﬁcients and are not constrained to equal 1.
We use continuous piecewise-linear or B-spline boundary basis functions rather than discontinuous
indicator patches when regularity and bounded gradients matter.
To discretize, let N be the number of interior potential nodes and B the number of boundary
potential nodes. Write vI ∈ RN for unknown interior values and vB ∈ RB for speciﬁed boundary
values. Let LII ∈ RN×N andLIB ∈ RN×B be the interior–interior and interior–boundary blocks
of a ﬁnite-difference or ﬁnite-element discretization of the Laplacian. Let C ∈ RB×M store the

5.1. Scope of the claims
The results above form an analytically consistent foundation; they are not yet a proved globally
convergent synthesis algorithm. In particular, existing harmonic theory explains the absence of
spurious interior extrema, standard stochastic analysis supplies the martingale and exit-time tools,
HJB gives outer feasibility bounds, and convexity follows only after ﬁxing a suitable time certiﬁ-
cate. Research-level advances remain to be demonstrated in certiﬁcate tightness, scalable global
parameter search, veriﬁed numerical complexity, and comparative performance against established
reach–avoid synthesis baselines.

require error bounds such as those discussed in Appendix C.

Any proposed implementation must still produce reliable residual and interpolation bounds, for

them.
C.3. Practical scope and open questions
The ﬁrst implementation may report accurate discrete-model probabilities and a separate continuum-
validation status. Claims of full continuous-domain guarantees should be made only after numerical
margins are established. Three key remaining research tasks are (i) low-cost repeated solves and
adjoints asθ changes, (ii) more informative joint risk/time bounds than a generic union bound, and
(iii) feasibility tests that scale beyond a handful of boundary coefﬁcients. This draft contains no
empirical comparison or claim of global synthesis convergence.
